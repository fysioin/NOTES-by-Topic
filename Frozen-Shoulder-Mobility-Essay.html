Physiotherapy Interventions in Musculoskeletal Disorders · Long essay

Discuss the interventions to improve the mobility of the shoulder joint following adhesive capsulitis

Paper IV (RS-4)  ·  Nov 2025  ·  QP Code 8132  ·  10 marks  ·  Tap any dotted term for a plain-language definition.

1. Introduction

Adhesive capsulitis ("frozen shoulder") is a stiff, painful shoulder with no other cause.

Definition: a condition of uncertain cause, with significant loss of both active and passive shoulder movement, in the absence of a known intrinsic shoulder disorder.
  • Affects 2–5% of adults.
  • Commonest between 40 and 70 years.
  • More common in women.
  • Linked to diabetes and thyroid disease.
  • Hallmark: near-total loss of external rotation.

Since the disability is a loss of movement, every intervention aims to relieve pain and restore movement.

2. Why mobility is lost

The joint capsule goes through a fixed sequence:

  • Inflammation of the capsule and synovium.
  • Fibrosis — scar tissue forms in the capsule.
  • Contracture — the capsule shrinks and tightens.

The tightening is worst at the front of the joint, around the rotator interval and coracohumeral ligament (CHL).

Capsular pattern: external rotation lost most → abduction → internal rotation least.

The condition passes through three overlapping stages, and the treatment must change with each one.

StageDurationMain problem
1 — Freezing (painful)2–9 monthsSevere pain, worse at night; motion starts to fall
2 — Frozen (adhesive)4–12 monthsPain eases; stiffness dominates
3 — Thawing5–26 monthsMovement returns gradually; shoulder is weak

3. Aims of intervention

  • Relieve pain — pain blocks movement, so it comes first.
  • Restore and preserve movement — the main goal, especially external rotation.
  • Rebuild strength and scapular control.
  • Restore function — daily activities, work, sport.
  • Educate and set realistic expectations.

4. Guiding principles

  • Symptom-limited: stretch below the pain threshold. Aggressive stretching makes it worse.
  • Match the stage: the right exercise differs in each phase.
  • Match the irritability: how easily pain flares decides how hard you push.
  • Multimodal: combined treatment beats any single method.

5. Interventions to improve mobility

5.1 Education and reassurance

  • Explain the natural history and that recovery is slow.
  • Reduces frustration and improves compliance.
  • Be honest: full range may not return.

5.2 Pain control — the prerequisite

  • NSAIDs first-line for pain.
  • Corticosteroid injection — best in the early freezing stage.
  • Heat before exercise, ice after.
  • TENS, ultrasound, cryotherapy as adjuncts.

5.3 Joint mobilisation and manual therapy

  • Accessory glides stretch the tight capsule.
  • Posterior and inferior glides are less painful and more effective than anterior glides.
  • Target the rotator interval and CHL to regain external rotation.
  • Irritable shoulder → mid-range; stiff shoulder → end-range mobilisation.

5.4 Stretching

  • Sustained self-stretch, hold 15–30 seconds.
  • Stay pain-free early; progress to "tolerable discomfort".
  • Stretch the chest muscles and the posterior capsule.
  • Regain rotation before elevation.

5.5 Range-of-motion exercises

  • Pendulum (Codman's) — gravity-assisted.
  • Wand/stick exercises — the good arm assists.
  • Pulley exercises for overhead movement.
  • Passive external rotation; active-assisted extension and internal rotation.
  • Home programme 3–5 times a day, with a stretch at end range.

5.6 Strengthening to hold the gain

  • Progress in order: isometric → Theraband → free weights.
  • Train the rotator cuff, deltoid and scapular stabilisers.
  • Correct the forward-shoulder posture.

5.7 Hydrodilatation

  • Hydrodilatation — fluid (saline + steroid + anaesthetic) is injected to stretch the capsule.
  • Used for the stiff stage; always followed by physiotherapy.

5.8 Surgery for refractory cases

  • Manipulation under anaesthesia (MUA) — the capsule is torn while asleep.
  • Arthroscopic capsular release — the capsule is cut under vision.
  • Both need immediate physiotherapy afterwards.

5.9 Precautions

  • Never stretch beyond the pain threshold — it worsens the fibrosis.
  • Avoid a forward-shoulder posture.
  • Rule out a cuff tear, osteoarthritis or calcific tendinitis first.
  • In diabetes and thyroid disease, recovery is slower.

6. Matching the intervention to the stage

This is the single most important decision.

StageFocusKey interventions
1 — FreezingPain relief; keep motionEducation, NSAIDs, steroid injection, heat/ice, gentle pendulum & wand ROM (1–5 s)
2 — FrozenRegain rangeSustained stretching, posterior/inferior glides, hydrodilatation, start isometrics
3 — ThawingRestore strengthMaintain ROM, progress isometric → Theraband → weights, scapular work
RefractoryAccelerate recoveryMUA or arthroscopic capsular release, then immediate physiotherapy

7. Evidence and expected outcome

  • Physiotherapy + NSAIDs beats NSAIDs alone.
  • Injection + physiotherapy beats injection alone.
  • Non-operative success reported up to 89.5%.
  • But 20–50% have lasting stiffness — external rotation worst.
  • Severe symptoms at onset, and diabetes, mean a worse course.

Track progress with goniometry (external rotation), a pain score, and shoulder function questionnaires (SPADI, DASH, ASES).

8. Conclusion

Improving mobility is not one technique but a staged, symptom-limited programme.

  • Control pain first — it blocks movement.
  • Restore range with gentle ROM work, targeted mobilisation, and progressive stretching.
  • Hold the gain with strengthening and posture.
  • Add hydrodilatation, and surgery only if refractory.
Because the tissue changes from inflammation → fibrosis → recovery, no single programme fits all three stages. Match the treatment to the stage and to the patient's irritability — and never push past the pain threshold.
