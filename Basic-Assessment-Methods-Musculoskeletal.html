Physical and Functional Diagnosis in Musculoskeletal Disorders · Long essay

Explain in detail the basic assessment methods for the diagnosis of musculoskeletal conditions

Paper III (RS-3)  ·  Long essay  ·  10 marks  ·  Structured on the orthopaedic physiotherapy evaluation form  ·  Tap any dotted term for a plain-language definition.

1. Introduction: the musculoskeletal system and its functions

The musculoskeletal system is the body's framework for movement. It is built from bones, joints, muscles, tendons, ligaments, fascia, cartilage and the nerves that drive and sense them. No single structure works alone: bone gives leverage, muscle gives force, tendon transmits it, the joint directs it, and the capsule and ligaments guide and restrain it.

Its basic functions are:

  • Support and posture — the skeleton holds the body upright against gravity.
  • Movement — muscles pull on bones across joints to produce purposeful motion.
  • Protection — the skull, rib cage and pelvis shield vital organs.
  • Stability and load transfer — joints, capsules and ligaments direct and absorb force.
  • Storage and metabolism — bone stores calcium and phosphate, and muscle is a protein and energy reserve.
  • Sensory feedback — proprioceptors in muscle, tendon and joint tell the brain where the body is in space.

When any of these structures is injured the result is pain, loss of movement, weakness or loss of function. Diagnosis means finding out which structure is at fault, how badly, and what it stops the patient doing. A musculoskeletal assessment is the systematic method by which the physiotherapist gathers that information.

2. Purpose and principles of assessment

The assessment is not a search for a label. Its purposes are to reach a functional diagnosis, to identify the tissue at fault and its stage, to detect red flags that need medical referral, to record a baseline for measuring progress, and to plan treatment.

Governing principles: take the history first and listen to the patient; test the normal side first; do active movements, then passive, then resisted isometric; do painful movements last; obtain informed consent and protect dignity; compare both sides and look for patterns; and refer on if the findings lie beyond your scope.

The whole process divides into two halves and a synthesis:

  • Subjective examination — the history, told by the patient.
  • Objective examination — what the examiner observes, feels and measures.
  • Synthesis — a problem list, a functional diagnosis, goals and a treatment plan.

3. Subjective examination (history)

This is the subjective examination — the patient's own account. With experience the examiner can often reach a working diagnosis from the history alone; observation and examination then confirm, alter or refute it.

3.1 Patient data and referral

Record name, age, gender, occupation, address and contact, registration number, civil status, and who referred the patient. Age and occupation are not trivial: the same shoulder pain means different things in a 25-year-old athlete and a 60-year-old sedentary worker, and the job tells you what postures and loads the patient must return to.

3.2 Chief complaints

The chief complaint is the patient's main problem in their own words, and how long it has troubled them. It sets the agenda for the whole examination.

3.3 History

  • Past medical history — diabetes, heart disease, hypertension, cancer, arthritis and previous surgery all change the picture, the prognosis and what treatment is safe.
  • Personal history — smoking, alcohol, activity and daily habits.
  • Family history — conditions such as arthritis and some cancers run in families.
  • Socioeconomic history — work demands, home circumstances, stress and support.

3.4 Symptom history

The symptoms are then defined precisely, because each answer narrows the list of possible structures:

  • Side and site — ask the patient to point. A specific point suggests a local lesion; a vague area suggests referral.
  • Onset — sudden (often trauma) or gradual (often overuse). Ask about the mechanism of injury — the direction and magnitude of the force — because it predicts which structures were hurt.
  • Duration and pattern — how long, and whether constant, periodic or episodic. Constant pain suggests chemical irritation or serious pathology; episodic pain suggests a mechanical cause.
  • Type and quality — sharp, shooting nerve pain; dull, aching muscle pain; deep, boring bone pain; burning, pressure-like sympathetic pain.
  • Severity and irritability — how intense the pain is, and how easily it flares with movement or posture.
  • Aggravating and relieving factors — which movements, positions, times of day and activities worsen or ease it.
  • Associated features — locking, giving way, instability, swelling, and any numbness, tingling or weakness.
Yellow flags: psychosocial factors — fear, anxiety, depression, job stress, or expectations of poor recovery — alter both the assessment and the treatment, and predict a poorer outcome. Screening tools such as the Fear-Avoidance Beliefs Questionnaire (FABQ) help to reveal them.

3.5 Pain evaluation

Pain is subjective and must be measured as objectively as possible, so that it can be compared over time:

  • A visual analogue scale (VAS) — the patient marks a point on a 10 cm line from "no pain" to "worst pain".
  • A numerical rating scale (0–10) or a verbal rating scale.
  • The McGill Pain Questionnaire, which uses sensory, affective and evaluative word descriptors.
  • The LANSS scale to judge whether neuropathic mechanisms dominate the pain.
  • A body chart on which the patient marks the location and spread of symptoms.

3.6 Vital signs

Vital signs establish the baseline physiological state and screen for conditions that affect treatment: temperature, heart rate, blood pressure and respiratory rate. Persistent abnormal readings warrant further investigation before exercise is prescribed.

4. Objective examination

The objective examination is what the examiner finds rather than what the patient reports. It begins before the patient undresses.

4.1 Observation

Observation is the "looking" phase, and it starts in the waiting room — how the patient sits, moves and guards the part before they know they are being watched. The patient is then adequately undressed, in private, and the examiner compares both sides:

  • General build, wasting and oedema — muscle bulk, swelling, and any bandages or scars.
  • Attitude of the limbs, bony contours and deformity — is there malalignment such as genu varum, a scoliosis, or a fixed flexion deformity?
  • Posture and alignment — anteriorly the nose, sternum and umbilicus should be in line; from the side the ear, acromion, iliac crest and lateral malleolus should align.
  • Skin — colour, temperature, trophic change, bruising and scars.
  • Gait — a first, cursory look that may already reveal a limp, a Trendelenburg sign or a drop foot.
  • Overt pain behaviour — guarding, bracing, rubbing, grimacing and sighing.

4.2 Palpation

Palpation confirms what observation suggested, using the hands to feel:

  • Tenderness — the exact site and severity.
  • Skin and tissue — temperature, texture, turgor, moisture and oedema.
  • Muscle — tone, spasm, trigger points and any gap or defect.
  • Swelling — and whether it is boggy, firm or pitting.
  • Bony landmarks and joint lines — for step deformities and tenderness.
  • Crepitus and abnormal sounds — which matter only when they reproduce the patient's symptoms.

4.3 Motor assessment

Range of motion (ROM)

Movement is tested in a fixed order. Active range of motion (AROM) is performed by the patient and tests joint range, muscle power, control and willingness to move together; the end of active movement is the physiological barrier. Passive range of motion (PROM) is performed by the examiner with the patient relaxed, and reveals the true available range and, critically, the end feel — the quality of resistance felt at the end of the range. Range is measured with a goniometer or inclinometer, and each movement is compared with the opposite side. A limitation that follows a fixed order across several directions is a capsular pattern.

Resisted isometric movements (RI)

Resisted isometric testing is done last, with the joint held in its resting position so that the inert tissues are under minimal tension. The patient contracts against resistance while movement is prevented ("don't let me move you"). Its purpose is to tell whether the contractile tissue — muscle, tendon and its bony attachment — is at fault. Four patterns emerge: strong and pain-free (normal), strong but painful (local muscle or tendon lesion), weak and painful (severe lesion such as fracture), and weak but pain-free (rupture or nerve involvement).

Reflexes

Superficial reflexes (abdominal, plantar) and deep tendon reflexes (biceps, brachioradialis, triceps, knee, ankle) are tested and compared side to side. A reflex that is diminished or absent points to a lower motor neuron or nerve-root lesion; an exaggerated reflex points to an upper motor neuron lesion.

Muscle girth and limb length

Muscle girth is measured at a fixed bony landmark and compared with the other side to quantify wasting. Limb length is measured to detect a true (bony) or apparent (postural) discrepancy that may be driving the problem elsewhere.

4.4 Sensory assessment

Sensation is mapped by testing dermatomes (the skin area supplied by one nerve root) with light touch, pin-prick and temperature, and myotomes (the muscles supplied by one nerve root) by resisted contraction held for at least five seconds. Loss that follows a dermatome or myotome points to a nerve-root lesion; loss that follows a peripheral nerve's territory points to a nerve-trunk lesion.

4.5 Functional assessment

Function is what matters most to the patient. The Functional Independence Measure (FIM) and similar tools score self-care, sphincter control, mobility, locomotion, communication and social adjustment, and measure the impact of the condition on activities of daily living (ADLs) such as feeding, dressing, hygiene, transfers and walking. It tells the examiner what the patient can and cannot do, and what they most want to regain.

4.6 Gait analysis

Walking is broken into the gait cycle — the stance phase, when the foot is on the ground, and the swing phase, when it is in the air. The examiner records step and stride length, base width, cadence, and any deviation, because the way a person walks often reveals the site and severity of the problem.

4.7 Balance

Static balance is tested sitting, standing and in tandem stance, with eyes open and closed; dynamic balance is tested with reaching activities and perturbations. Poor balance signals impaired proprioception or weakness, and a risk of falls.

4.8 Hand function

For the upper limb, the examiner observes reaching, grasping and releasing, and notes any assistive devices the patient uses, since the hand is the organ of daily function.

4.9 Coordination

Coordination is tested by smooth, rapid, alternating movements and by finger-to-nose testing; clumsiness or an inability to perform rapid alternating movements (dysdiadochokinesia) points to a cerebellar or neurological problem.

4.10 Special tests

A special test is a specific manoeuvre for one joint that stresses one structure to confirm or exclude a particular condition — for example an apprehension test for shoulder instability or a McMurray test for a meniscal tear. Each is recorded as positive or negative, and is meaningful only in the context of the whole assessment.

4.11 Investigations

Imaging and laboratory findings are interpreted alongside the clinical picture, never instead of it: plain radiographs for bone and alignment, MRI for soft tissue, CT for complex bone detail, and electromyography (EMG) and nerve conduction studies for nerve lesions.

5. From findings to diagnosis

The assessment ends by pulling the findings together:

  • Problem list — each impairment (e.g. loss of external rotation), the functional limitation it causes (e.g. cannot reach overhead), and the resulting disability (e.g. cannot work).
  • Functional diagnosis — not just the name of the condition, but which tissue is at fault, its stage and its irritability.
  • Goals — short-term and long-term, agreed with the patient.
  • Treatment plan and a home programme, with a date and the therapist's signature.
A diagnosis is not made on the strength of the first positive finding. It is made only when the picture is complete and there are no contradictory signs — which is why the examination must be systematic and extensive enough for a pattern to emerge.

6. Conclusion

The basic assessment of a musculoskeletal condition is a disciplined sequence, not a collection of tests. It begins with a careful history that often points to the diagnosis, moves through observation and a systematic examination of movement, strength, sensation and function, uses special tests and investigations to confirm or exclude specific conditions, and ends by converting the findings into a problem list and a functional diagnosis. Because the tissues differ in how they behave — contractile tissue hurts on contraction, inert tissue on stretch, nervous tissue along its path — the examiner who follows the same thorough routine every time is the one who finds the structure at fault, spots the red flags, and treats the patient safely and effectively.

Glossary of difficult terms (17)

Dotted-underlined words also show the definition on hover or tap.

  • capsular pattern — The characteristic order in which a joint loses range when the whole capsule is involved.
  • capsule — The fibrous sleeve that surrounds and stabilises a joint.
  • dermatome — The area of skin supplied by a single spinal nerve root.
  • fear-avoidance — Avoiding activity because of fear of pain, which over time worsens disability.
  • gait — The manner of walking.
  • goniometry — Measuring joint angles with a goniometer.
  • isometric — A muscle contraction with no change in length (no joint movement).
  • manual muscle testing — Grading a muscle's strength by resisting a voluntary contraction (abbreviated MMT).
  • myotome — The group of muscles supplied by a single spinal nerve root.
  • oedema — Swelling caused by fluid trapped in the tissues.
  • palpation — Examining the body by touch to feel for tenderness, swelling or tissue change.
  • posture — The position of the body and its parts relative to gravity.
  • proprioception — The sense of body and joint position and movement.
  • range of motion — How far a joint can move (abbreviated ROM).
  • ROM — Short for range of motion - how far a joint can move.
  • special test — A clinical test used to confirm or rule out a specific structure or condition.
  • tendon — Strong fibrous tissue that attaches muscle to bone.
