How do you conclude during screening that the pain in the lumbar spine is due to disc herniation?

Justified with differential diagnosis  ·  Paper III (RS-3)  ·  Long essay  ·  10 marks  ·  Simple drawable diagrams  ·  Tap any dotted term for a plain-language definition.

1. Introduction

A lumbar disc herniation is the displacement of nucleus pulposus, or of annulus material, beyond the normal margin of the intervertebral disc. Because the posterior and posterolateral annulus is the weakest part of the disc, the extruded material most often tracks posterolaterally, where it contacts or compresses a traversing or exiting nerve root. The result is low back pain with or without radicular leg pain (sciatica).

The screening question is never simply "is there a disc bulge?" Imaging shows disc bulges in 20–30% of symptom-free adults, so a bulge on a scan does not prove that the disc is generating the pain. The real question is whether the patient's pain is produced by a mechanically and chemically irritated nerve root, and whether the whole clinical picture fits a disc lesion rather than another structure.

Diagnosis in physiotherapy is pattern recognition plus exclusion. No single test is both highly sensitive and highly specific, so the conclusion is reached by building a consistent cluster of findings and by showing that the competing diagnoses do not fit.

2. Functional anatomy and pathomechanism

The disc has a tough outer annulus fibrosus and a gelatinous inner nucleus pulposus. A posterolateral herniation compresses the root at that level: a paracentral L4–L5 herniation typically affects the L5 root, and a paracentral L5–S1 herniation typically affects the S1 root. Radicular pain arises from mechanical compression plus chemical and inflammatory irritation of the root and its dorsal root ganglion.

Nerve roots are relatively fixed as they cross the intervertebral foramen, so any movement of the spine or limb that increases root tension reproduces the leg pain. That single fact is the physiological basis of the neural tension tests — straight leg raise and slump — on which the screening conclusion rests.

3. The screening process — gathering the evidence

3.1 History (the strongest single element)

  • Mechanism — a quick movement into flexion, rotation, side flexion or extension, often while bending and lifting or twisting under load.
  • Pain pattern — back pain with referral into the posterior or lateral leg and foot, in a band that follows a nerve root; the leg pain is often worse than the back pain.
  • Aggravating factors — coughing, sneezing, straining and sitting increase pain (the Valsalva manoeuvre raises intradiscal and dural pressure).
  • Relieving factors — lying down; some patients ease in extension, others in flexion (their directional preference).
  • Associated features — numbness, tingling and weakness, plus the red-flag questions (saddle numbness, bladder or bowel disturbance, bilateral symptoms, progressive weakness, night pain, weight loss, cancer history, fever, significant trauma, new pain over age 50).

3.2 Observation

Look for an antalgic (sciatic) scoliosis or lateral shift, muscle guarding, and an inability to stand straight. Loss of the lumbar lordosis and an asymmetric, protective posture that reduces root tension are typical.

3.3 Active and passive movement

There is pain on both extension and flexion, with side flexion and rotation also affected, and overall range is limited. The meaningful finding is a movement that reproduces the leg pain, not merely back pain. Repeated-movement examination may show centralisation of leg pain (a favourable disc sign) or peripheralisation (a worsening sign).

3.4 Neurological examination — localising the root

  • Myotomes — L4 ankle dorsiflexion (tibialis anterior), L5 great-toe and foot dorsiflexion (extensor hallucis longus), S1 plantar flexion (gastrocnemius–soleus).
  • Dermatomes — L4 medial leg, L5 lateral leg and dorsum of the foot and great toe, S1 lateral foot and sole.
  • Reflexes — L4 knee jerk, S1 ankle jerk (L5 has no reliable reflex).

A dermatomal sensory loss, a single myotomal weakness and a matching diminished reflex together point to one nerve root.

3.5 Neural tension tests — the most useful provocation tests

  • Straight leg raise (Lasègue) — hip flexion with the knee extended; a positive test reproduces the patient's leg pain at about 30–70°. Pain is confirmed by sensitising manoeuvres: ankle dorsiflexion (Bragard), neck flexion (Brudzinski / Soto-Hall), or medial hip rotation. The L5, S1 and S2 roots are fully stretched by about 70° of hip flexion, with an excursion of roughly 2–6 cm.
  • Crossed (well-leg) SLR (Fajersztajn) — raising the unaffected leg reproduces pain in the affected leg; this is highly specific for a space-occupying lesion such as a herniated disc.
  • Slump test — seated, the patient slumps the thoracic and lumbar spine, flexes the neck, then extends the knee and dorsiflexes the ankle. Reproduction of radicular pain that eases when the neck or the knee is released is a positive test. It adds cephalad dural glide, so it may be positive even when the SLR is negative.
  • Femoral nerve traction test — for upper lumbar (L2–L4) lesions, performed prone by extending the hip and flexing the knee.

3.6 Investigations

MRI is the investigation of choice for soft tissue such as disc and root; CT shows bone detail; plain radiographs mainly exclude fracture or other bony pathology; and EMG / nerve conduction studies confirm radiculopathy and exclude a peripheral nerve lesion. Imaging is always interpreted with the clinical picture, never instead of it.

4. How the conclusion is reached — the diagnostic cluster

The clinician concludes that the pain is due to disc herniation when a consistent cluster is present:

  1. A history of radicular pain in a dermatomal distribution, worse on Valsalva and eased by rest.
  2. A positive neural tension sign (SLR and/or slump) that reproduces the patient's leg pain.
  3. A matching, single-root neurological deficit (dermatome, myotome or reflex).
  4. Findings that localise to one level and one side.
  5. Absence of features pointing to another structure, and absence of red flags.
  6. Confirmation on MRI that correlates with the clinical level.
The SLR is sensitive but not specific. A negative SLR does not exclude a disc lesion, and a positive SLR does not by itself prove one. It is the combination — radicular history + positive neural tension test + matching root deficit + a normal competing examination — that raises the probability enough to conclude, and imaging then confirms it.

The essential skill is to show that the picture fits a disc lesion and not one of its mimics. The table below contrasts the two commonest presentations in the lumbar region.

Other differentials and how each is distinguished

  • Non-specific (mechanical) low back pain / strain — no radicular pain, normal neurology, negative neural tension tests.
  • Facet (zygapophyseal) joint arthropathy — pain on extension and rotation, paraspinal and referred non-dermatomally, with no neurological deficit.
  • Sacroiliac joint dysfunction — pain below L5 over the PSIS and buttock, provoked by SI cluster tests; neurology normal.
  • Lumbar spinal stenosis — neurogenic claudication, bilateral, worse on walking and standing, relieved by sitting or flexion; usually older patients; SLR often negative.
  • Spondylolysis / spondylolisthesis — extension pain, positive one-leg standing (stork) test; imaging confirms the pars defect or slip.
  • Myofascial pain / trigger points — referred pain that does not follow a dermatome, no neurological deficit.
  • Hip pathology (e.g. osteoarthritis) — groin or buttock pain, restricted hip range, positive hip quadrant / scour. A positive sign of the buttock (SLR limited by buttock pain that does not change with ankle dorsiflexion) points to hip or buttock pathology, not a disc.
  • Piriformis syndrome — buttock pain with a positive SLR but normal neurology; the source is the muscle, not the root.
  • Vertebral compression fracture — sudden onset in an osteoporotic or older patient, focal tenderness, red flags; imaging.
  • Infection (discitis, spinal tuberculosis) and malignancy — constant unrelenting night pain, fever, weight loss, cancer history, new pain over age 50 — red flags that mandate referral, not physiotherapy.
  • Ankylosing spondylitis — young patient, insidious onset, morning stiffness, bilateral SI involvement, improves with exercise.
  • Cauda equina syndrome — saddle anaesthesia, bladder or bowel dysfunction, bilateral leg symptoms, progressive weakness — a surgical emergency.

7. Red flags — when NOT to conclude "disc"

Before attributing the pain to a disc, screen for and exclude serious pathology: new pain over age 50, significant trauma, night pain that wakes the patient, unexplained weight loss, a history of cancer, fever, intravenous drug use, immunosuppression, saddle anaesthesia, and bladder or bowel dysfunction. Their presence changes the conclusion from "disc herniation" to "refer for urgent medical assessment".

8. Conclusion

The pain in the lumbar spine is concluded to be due to disc herniation when a radicular history is combined with a positive neural tension test that reproduces the leg pain, a matching single-root neurological deficit, and the absence of any competing diagnosis or red flag — with MRI confirming the level. Screening is a process of exclusion as much as detection: the SLR is sensitive but not specific, the crossed SLR and the slump test add information, and no single finding is decisive. The examiner therefore concludes from the whole pattern, not from one test, and justifies the diagnosis by showing that the differentials — strain, facet and SI joint dysfunction, stenosis, spondylolisthesis, myofascial pain, hip pathology, fracture, infection, tumour and cauda equina — do not fit.

Glossary of difficult terms (12)

Dotted-underlined words also show the definition on hover or tap.

  • dermatome — The area of skin supplied by a single spinal nerve root.
  • dorsiflexion — Bending the ankle or wrist so the angle at the front decreases (e.g. lifting the toes up).
  • isometric — A muscle contraction with no change in length (no joint movement).
  • myofascial — Relating to muscle and its surrounding fascia.
  • myotome — The group of muscles supplied by a single spinal nerve root.
  • posture — The position of the body and its parts relative to gravity.
  • radiculopathy — A nerve-root problem causing pain, numbness or weakness along a limb.
  • red flag — A sign or symptom suggesting serious pathology that needs medical referral.
  • ROM — Short for range of motion - how far a joint can move.
  • sensitivity — A test's ability to correctly identify people who have the condition.
  • specificity — A test's ability to correctly identify people who do not have the condition.
  • traction — A gentle pulling force applied to a joint or the spine to relieve pressure.
